<Beat n={1}>

This lesson plays on one small **board**: three **nodes** in a row, joined by **roads**. The board is called <Tex src="A_3"/>, and its nodes carry the labels <Tex src="1"/>, <Tex src="2"/> and <Tex src="3"/> from left to right. A line between two nodes stands for one road running each way.

In the first game the nodes are **cities** on Mars. Each city holds a whole number, its **population**: a positive number counts Martians, a negative one counts anti-Martians. The game and its story are N. J. Wildberger's, retold here in our own words (<Cite k="W20"/>, section two). The figure starts with one Martian in the middle city, the population <Tex src="p=(0,1,0)"/>.

To **mutate** city $i$, the inhabitants of city $i$ change type, and every other city sends one copy of its population down each road that leads into city $i$. Only the entry $p_i$ changes, and the board itself is never changed by a mutation. With $A_{ji}$ the number of roads from city $j$ into city $i$, the rule reads

$$
p_i \longmapsto -p_i + \sum_{j \ne i} A_{ji}\, p_j .
$$

Mutate city <Tex src="1"/> in the figure. It holds nothing, and it receives the single Martian of city <Tex src="2"/> down the one road between them, so it ends with <Tex src="-0+1=1"/>. Every other city keeps its population.

<Beat n={2}>

After that move the population is <Tex src="(1,1,0)"/>. City <Tex src="2"/> holds one Martian, and its neighbours hold one and none. Before you touch the next board, predict what city <Tex src="2"/> will hold after it mutates. The board stays still until you answer or ask to be shown.

City <Tex src="2"/> changes type, which turns its Martian into an anti-Martian, and it receives one Martian from city <Tex src="1"/> and nothing from city <Tex src="3"/>. So it holds <Tex src="-1+1+0=0"/>, and the population becomes <Tex src="(1,0,0)"/>. A mutation can empty a city.

<Beat n={3}>

The second game keeps the <Term a="board">board</Term> and changes the record. In the <strong><View v="numbers"/></strong> view each node holds an integer, simply called its number, and the list of numbers is a **position**. To **fire** node $i$, node $i$ sends its number down every road that leaves it, then changes sign:

$$
\begin{aligned} a_i &\longmapsto -a_i, \\ a_j &\longmapsto a_j + A_{ij}\, a_i \quad (j \ne i). \end{aligned}
$$

The figure starts at the position <Tex src="(0,1,0)"/>. Firing node <Tex src="2"/> sends its <Tex src="1"/> to node <Tex src="1"/> and to node <Tex src="3"/> and leaves <Tex src="-1"/> behind, so the position becomes <Tex src="(1,-1,1)"/>. A firing changes the fired node and its neighbours; a mutation changes one city only.

This figure is in **free play**, where any node may fire whatever the sign of its number, and the board names the mode in force. The game proper is **legal play**: a node may fire only when its number is positive, and the game ends when no number is positive. Legal play returns in a later module.

The numbers game is due to Mozes <Cite k="Moz90"/>. Proctor used the same process earlier <Cite k="Pro84"/>, and Eriksson developed it <Cite k="Eri92"/>. Authors disagree on which sign a legal move fires; this site fires positive numbers, as Proctor, Donnelly and Wildberger do. In Wildberger's story the second record belongs to Venus, the planet of his dual game <Cite k="W-MS"/>.

</Beat>

<Beat n={4}>

Now both records share one figure: the <View v="populations"/> board above, the <View v="numbers"/> board below. They are tied together by the **translator** $a=Cp$. Here $C$ is the **Cartan matrix** of the board, <Tex src="C=2I-A^{\mathsf T}"/>, with twos on the diagonal and minus the road counts off it. On <Tex src="A_3"/> its rows are <G k="types.A3.cartan[0]"/>, <G k="types.A3.cartan[1]"/> and <G k="types.A3.cartan[2]"/>. One Martian in the middle city therefore has the numbers <G k="types.A3.cartan[1]"/>, and the <View v="numbers"/> board shows exactly these.

What do these numbers say about the populations? Answer the figure's question first, then read on.

<p data-bridge-prose><strong>The number on node <i>i</i> is the amount by which a mutation would lower city <i>i</i>.</strong></p>

In symbols, a mutation at $i$ replaces $p_i$ by $p_i-a_i$. A positive number marks a mutation that lowers, zero a mutation that changes nothing, and a negative number a mutation that raises. Here the middle node shows <Tex src="2"/>. So mutating city <Tex src="2"/> takes it from <Tex src="1"/> down to <Tex src="-1"/>.

Acting on either board acts on both. After the move the <Term a="population">populations</Term> read <Tex src="(0,-1,0)"/> and the numbers read <Tex src="(1,-2,1)"/>. That second list is exactly what firing node <Tex src="2"/> does to <Tex src="(-1,2,-1)"/>. One move, two records.

The two games are still not the same moves: a mutation changes one entry, a firing changes an entry and its neighbours. What holds is that the translator carries one onto the other. Write the mutation at $i$ as a matrix $S_i$ and the firing at $i$ as a matrix $R_i$; then $CS_i=R_iC$, so mutating and then translating gives the same as translating and then firing. This identity is verified by computation on every board of <Term a="finite">finite type</Term> of rank at most eight. The boards of finite type form a short list, which contains <Tex src="A_3"/>; module three explains which boards belong to it.

Wildberger states the same link with the opposite sign. His dual population records the gain a mutation would cause, $q=-Cp$, where this site records the drop <Cite k="W-MS"/>. The site uses $a=Cp$ so that the numbers are the standard labels named in the paragraph for readers who know Lie theory. Every move is linear, so the two choices give the same games.

<Beat n={5}>

The rest of the site grows out of these two moves. It rests on seven statements, and each module develops one or two of them. Some statements use standard names that a later module explains: roots, weights, the Weyl group, a Lie algebra, a real form and a Satake diagram.

<figure slot="figure" class="statements">
<ol aria-label="The seven statements of the site" data-statements>
<li>A Dynkin diagram describes a set of mirrors through one point: one mirror per node, the roads giving the angles between mirrors and an arrow giving a difference in scale.<Developed ns={[6, 7]} /></li>
<li>Reflect a point in one mirror. Recorded by its coordinates along the mirror normals, the point changes in one coordinate only, which is a mutation. Recorded by its signed distances to the mirrors, the distance to the chosen mirror changes sign and its neighbours absorb the change, which is a firing.<Developed ns={[1, 4]} /></li>
<li>The Cartan matrix converts the first record into the second. Its entries are the road counts of the board, and it is the one piece of algebra that both games carry.<Developed ns={[4]} /></li>
<li>Playing is walking in the Weyl group. The populations reached from one Martian are roots, positions are weights, legal play spells reduced words, and the numbers fired along the way lead to the exponents, the order of the group and Weyl's dimension formula.<Developed ns={[5]} /></li>
<li>The board alone decides whether the game closes up. It does so exactly when a quadratic form of the board is positive definite, which singles out the Dynkin diagrams; on the boundary sit the <Term a="affine">affine</Term> diagrams, with one frozen population and one conserved weighted sum.<Developed ns={[2, 3]} /></li>
<li>A simple Lie algebra appears when each reflection is cut into single steps up and down. In the smallest representations every step is a move of the game, and the graph of the game is the representation.<Developed ns={[8]} /></li>
<li>A real form is the same algebra seen through an involution. On the board it is a choice of black nodes and arrows, a Satake diagram; the involution can itself be played, and the shadows it casts on the roots form a smaller board with a game of its own.<Developed ns={[9]} /></li>
</ol>

<LieTheory>

A population $p$ stands for the element $\sum_i p_i\alpha_i$ of the root lattice $Q$, written in simple-root coordinates, and a <Term a="mutate">mutation</Term> at $i$ is the simple reflection $s_i(\beta)=\beta-\langle\alpha_i^\vee,\beta\rangle\alpha_i$. The <Term a="numbers">numbers</Term> are the Dynkin labels $a_i=\langle\lambda,\alpha_i^\vee\rangle$ of a weight $\lambda=\sum_i a_i\omega_i$, and a <Term a="fire">firing</Term> at $i$ is the same reflection, $s_i\lambda=\lambda-a_i\alpha_i$, in fundamental-weight coordinates, because $\alpha_i=\sum_j C_{ji}\,\omega_j$. Here $C$ is the Cartan matrix in the convention $C_{ij}=\langle\alpha_i^\vee,\alpha_j\rangle$ of Kac and SageMath; Bourbaki and Humphreys print its transpose. The <Term a="the-translator">translator</Term> $a=Cp$ is the inclusion of $Q$ in the weight lattice $P$, of index $\det C$, which is <G k="types.A3.det_cartan"/> on <Tex src="A_3"/>. The <Term a="board">board</Term> <Tex src="A_3"/> is the Dynkin diagram of type <Tex src="A_3"/> in Bourbaki's numbering, and the start <Tex src="(0,1,0)"/> of the <View v="numbers"/> figure is the fundamental weight <Tex src="\omega_2"/>.

</LieTheory>
