<Beat n={1}>

Module zero showed the same board in a second record, the **Numbers** view. Here that record becomes a game of its own. A **position** is a list of whole numbers, one on each node of the board. To **fire** node $i$, the node sends its number down every road that leaves it, then changes sign:

$$
\begin{aligned} a_i &\longmapsto -a_i, \\ a_j &\longmapsto a_j - C_{ji}\, a_i \quad (j \ne i). \end{aligned}
$$

Here $C$ is the Cartan matrix met in module zero, so $-C_{ji}$ is the number of roads from node $i$ to node $j$. In **free play** any node may fire. In **legal play**, the game proper, a node may fire only when its number is positive, and the game ends when no number is positive; a zero cannot be fired. The site offers both and every figure names the one in force. The figure below is in legal play.

Take <Tex src="A_2"/>, two nodes joined by a road, from <Tex src="(1,1)"/>. Firing node <Tex src="1"/> gives <Tex src="(-1,2)"/>. Now only node <Tex src="2"/> is positive, and firing it gives <Tex src="(1,-2)"/>; firing node <Tex src="1"/> again gives <Tex src="(-1,-1)"/>. No number is positive, so the game is over after <G k="types.A2.n_positive_roots"/> moves. The nodes fired were <G k="types.A2.longest_word" as="word"/> and the numbers fired <G k="types.A2.fired_values"/>. Starting at node <Tex src="2"/> instead also takes <G k="types.A2.n_positive_roots"/> moves.

The numbers game is Mozes' <Cite k="Moz90"/>. Proctor used the same process earlier <Cite k="Pro84"/>, and Eriksson developed it <Cite k="Eri95b"/>. Which sign a legal move fires is a convention. This site follows Proctor, Donnelly and Wildberger and fires positive numbers; Eriksson, the Olympiad problem of beat six, and Gashi, Schedler and Speyer fire negative ones. Negating every number translates one convention into the other, because each firing is linear.

<Beat n={2}>

Now three nodes in a row, <Tex src="A_3"/>, from <Tex src="(1,1,1)"/>. Every node is positive at the start, so you choose where to begin. Before you play, answer the question on the board: will another order of moves give another ending, or another number of moves? Then play to the end, reset, and play again in a different order.

Every order lasts <G k="types.A3.n_positive_roots"/> moves and ends at <Tex src="(-1,-1,-1)"/>. The game that always fires the lowest positive node fires the nodes <G k="types.A3.longest_word" as="word"/> and the numbers <G k="types.A3.fired_values"/>.

Two facts lie behind this. First, on these boards any two legal plays from the same start end at the same position, after the same number of moves, and fire the same numbers, perhaps in another order. Eriksson calls a game with this property strongly convergent (<Cite k="Eri95b"/>, {'Definition 1.1'}); the theorem is attributed to Mozes and Eriksson, and Donnelly proves it in this site's sign convention (<Cite k="Don08"/>, {'Theorem 2.1'}). Second, from a start with every number positive, every legal play has as many moves as the board has positive root populations, and it ends at the start with every sign changed and the entries permuted by a symmetry of the board. On <Tex src="A_3"/> that symmetry reverses the nodes, <G k="types.A3.minus_w0" as="word"/>. This was verified by computation on every board of finite type of rank at most eight.

The Numbers Game Lab plays a second game beside the first, always at the highest positive node: <Tex src="3,2,3,1,2,3"/>. The two games make different moves and reach the same end in the same number of moves. Each keeps a tape of the numbers it fired. Sorted, the two tapes agree; it is the sorted tapes that must agree, not the order of firing.

<Beat n={3}>

Module zero tied the two records together, and this board puts the tie to work. It shows <Tex src="A_3"/> with the population <Tex src="(1,1,0)"/> on the Populations board above and its numbers on the Numbers board below. The **translator** $a=Cp$ computes them, entry by entry, as <Tex src="a_i=2p_i-\sum_{j\ne i}A_{ji}\,p_j"/>. So the Numbers board shows <Tex src="(1,1,-1)"/>.

<p data-bridge-prose><strong>The number on node <i>i</i> is the amount by which a mutation would lower city <i>i</i>.</strong></p>

A positive number marks a mutation that lowers, zero a mutation that changes nothing, and a negative number a mutation that raises. In symbols, a mutation at $i$ replaces $p_i$ by $p_i-a_i$. Acting on either board acts on both. Make three moves anywhere on the figure, and after each one check that every number is still the drop a mutation would cause.

The two moves still differ: a mutation changes one entry, a firing changes an entry and its neighbours. What holds is $CS_i=R_iC$, where $S_i$ and $R_i$ are the matrices of the two moves. Mutating and then translating gives the same as translating and then firing; this is verified by computation on every board of finite type of rank at most eight. Wildberger states the same link with the opposite sign: his dual population is $q=-Cp$, the gain a mutation would cause, where this site records the drop (<Cite k="W-MS"/>, {'Proposition 0.3, pp. 31-32'}). In his story the second record belongs to Venus (<Cite k="W-MS"/>, {'p. 30'}).

<Beat n={4}>

Why should one matrix translate one game into the other? The **Kaleidoscope** draws <Tex src="A_2"/> as a picture in the plane. Each node is a mirror through the origin, and its arrow, perpendicular to the mirror, is the mirror's normal. A point of the plane has two sets of coordinates. Its population lists its coefficients along the two normals. Its numbers are its distances to the two mirrors, each scaled: <Tex src="a_i=\tfrac{2}{|\alpha_i|}\times\bigl(\text{signed distance to mirror } i\bigr)"/>.

Firing node $i$ reflects the point in mirror $i$. One reflection, recorded two ways. The Cartan matrix holds the pairings between normals and mirrors, which is why it is the translator.

The figure starts at the numbers <Tex src="(1,1)"/>, whose population on this board is <Tex src="(1,1)"/> too. Fire node <Tex src="1"/>: the point crosses mirror <Tex src="1"/>, the numbers become <Tex src="(-1,2)"/> and the population <Tex src="(0,1)"/>. Only the first coefficient changed, and it fell by the number fired.

</Beat>

<Beat n={5}>

The translation runs backwards too: the population of a position is <Tex src="C^{-1}a"/>. On <Tex src="A_2"/> the position <Tex src="(1,0)"/> gives <Tex src="\bigl(\tfrac23,\tfrac13\bigr)"/>: two thirds of a Martian in city <Tex src="1"/> and one third in city <Tex src="2"/>. No population of whole Martians has these numbers, so not every position is a population.

Both sets are infinite, and the comparison is made by classes. The translator carries the populations onto part of the positions, and the positions fall into $\det C$ classes, each a shifted copy of the populations; on <Tex src="A_2"/>, $\det C$ is <G k="types.A2.det_cartan"/>. The denominators of <Tex src="C^{-1}a"/> always divide $\det C$. On <Tex src="E_8"/>, <Tex src="F_4"/> and <Tex src="G_2"/>, where $\det C$ is <G k="types.E8.det_cartan"/>, every position is the translation of exactly one population, as verified by computation.

The start <Tex src="(1,0)"/> is the **fundamental position** of node <Tex src="1"/>. Fire from it in free play: node <Tex src="1"/> gives <Tex src="(-1,1)"/>, then node <Tex src="2"/> gives <Tex src="(0,-1)"/>, and no other position ever appears. The garden beside the board adds each position the first time the board reaches it. The orbit of <Tex src="(1,0)"/> has <G k="types.A2.fundamental_orbit_sizes[0]"/> positions, and every number in it is <Tex src="-1"/>, <Tex src="0"/> or <Tex src="1"/>. A node whose fundamental position has an orbit like this is called **minuscule**; on <Tex src="A_2"/> the minuscule nodes are <G k="types.A2.minuscule_nodes"/>, which is every node.

<Beat n={6}>

The game has an ancestor in a competition puzzle, problem three of the International Mathematical Olympiad of {'1986'} <Cite k="IMO86"/>. Put five whole numbers with a positive sum on the corners of a pentagon. While some number is negative, pick one, change its sign and add it to both of its neighbours. Must this process stop, whatever choices are made?

This is the numbers game on the cycle of five nodes, played with the opposite sign: the puzzle fires a negative number where this site fires a positive one. Negating every number translates one convention into the other, so the figure starts in the Olympiad's own form, with a switch to this site's. The problem prescribes no numbers. The site's example start is <G k="pentagon_example.start_olympiad_convention"/> in the Olympiad's convention, which is <G k="pentagon_example.start"/> in this site's. Try to make it last, then press play on. It ends after <G k="pentagon_example.moves"/> moves whatever the order of play: all <G k="pentagon_example.complete_games"/> complete games were enumerated by computation.

<Beat n={7}>

Why must it stop? The pentagon is one of the boundary boards of module three: the cycle of five nodes is the affine board <Tex src="\tilde A_4"/>. Besides its frozen population, such a board has positive weights on its nodes called **comarks**, the vector $c$ with <Tex src="c^{\mathsf T}C=0"/>. The **level** of a position is the weighted sum $\ell(a)=\sum_i c_i a_i$, and no firing changes it, as verified by computation. On the pentagon the comarks are <G k="affine.A4.comarks"/>, so the level is the plain sum of the numbers: <G k="pentagon_example.level"/> for the example. A positive sum in the Olympiad's convention is a negative level here.

The sign of the level decides between three fates:

On affine boards with single roads, such as the pentagon, this is a theorem of Gashi, Schedler and Speyer, credited there to Eriksson and stated with the signs reversed, because they fire negative numbers (<Cite k="GSS12"/>, {'Theorem 1.2'}). <Unverified item="E6" fallback="On boards with multiple roads the same table was computed on every affine board the site offers, and the Affine Playground gives an argument for it beside it.">The same table holds on the affine boards with multiple roads, which arrive in module six.</Unverified>

Play detects two of the three fates exactly: a game that has ended, and a position that has come back. That a game of positive level never ends cannot be seen by playing. The playground stops at {'20000'} moves and says so; that is the limit of the search, not a proof.

In the figure, change one number of the start and watch the level and the sentence beside it. Raising node <Tex src="5"/> from <Tex src="1"/> to <Tex src="2"/> makes the level zero; press play on, and the game comes back to a position it has already visited.

<LieTheory>

<Term a="position">Positions</Term> are weights $\lambda=\sum_i a_i\omega_i$ in fundamental-weight coordinates, and the <Term a="numbers">numbers</Term> are the Dynkin labels $a_i=\langle\lambda,\alpha_i^\vee\rangle$. To <Term a="fire">fire</Term> node $i$ is to apply the simple reflection $s_i\lambda=\lambda-a_i\alpha_i$, because $\alpha_i=\sum_j C_{ji}\,\omega_j$ is column $i$ of $C$. <Term a="legal-play">Legal play</Term> descends from the dominant chamber towards the antidominant one; from $\rho$ it ends at <Tex src="w_0\rho=-\rho"/> after $N$ moves, $N$ the number of positive roots. <Term a="the-translator">The translator</Term> $a=Cp$ is $\lambda\mapsto(\langle\lambda,\alpha_i^\vee\rangle)_i$, the inclusion of the root lattice $Q$ in the weight lattice $P$, and $P/Q$, of order $\det C$, is the fundamental group of the adjoint group. A <Term a="fundamental-position">fundamental position</Term> is a fundamental weight; <Tex src="(1,0)"/> on <Tex src="A_2"/> is the minuscule weight <Tex src="\omega_1"/>. The <Term a="level">level</Term> is $\langle\lambda,K\rangle$, the comarks being the coefficients of the canonical central element $K$. Here $C_{ij}=\langle\alpha_i^\vee,\alpha_j\rangle$, as in Kac and SageMath; Bourbaki prints the transpose. Donnelly's matrix $M$ is $C^{\mathsf T}$, and Gashi, Schedler and Speyer write $c=C^{\mathsf T}$ and fire negative numbers.

</LieTheory>
