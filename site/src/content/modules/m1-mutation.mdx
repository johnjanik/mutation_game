<Beat n={1}>

The mutation game is N. J. Wildberger's, told as a story about Mars <Cite k="W20"/>; this lesson retells it in its own words. Mars has **cities**, and some pairs of cities are joined by **roads**. Each city holds a whole number, its **population**: a positive population counts **Martians**, a negative one counts **anti-Martians**, and zero means the <Term a="city">city</Term> is empty. The cities and <Term a="roads">roads</Term> together are the **board**.

The <Term a="board">board</Term> <Tex src="A_2"/> has two cities joined by one road each way. Put one Martian in city <Tex src="1"/> and nobody in city <Tex src="2"/>: the population is <Tex src="(1,0)"/>.

To **mutate** a city, its inhabitants change type, and every other city sends one copy of its population down each road that leads into the mutated city. No other city changes, and the board itself is never changed by a mutation: the roads stay as they are. With $A_{ji}$ the number of roads from city $j$ into city $i$, the rule reads

$$
p_i \longmapsto -p_i + \sum_{j \ne i} A_{ji}\, p_j .
$$

A worked play. <Term a="mutate">Mutate</Term> city <Tex src="2"/>: it holds nobody, so changing type changes nothing, and it receives the one Martian of city <Tex src="1"/>. The population becomes <Tex src="(1,1)"/>. Now mutate city <Tex src="1"/>: its Martian becomes an anti-Martian, and city <Tex src="2"/> sends one Martian down the road, so city <Tex src="1"/> holds <Tex src="-1+1=0"/> and the population is <Tex src="(0,1)"/>.

Before you play on, predict how many different populations you can reach from <Tex src="(1,0)"/> by mutations in any order. The board waits for your answer, and the garden below it records each population the board reaches. There are exactly <G k="types.A2.n_roots"/>, and you can find them all.

<Beat n={2}>

These populations have names. One Martian in city $i$ and nobody anywhere else is a **simple root population**, written $e_i$. A **root population** is any population that mutations reach from a <Term a="simple-root-population">simple root population</Term>, so the populations of beat one are the root populations of <Tex src="A_2"/>. A <Term a="root-population">root population</Term> is **positive** when no city holds <Term a="anti-martians">anti-Martians</Term>.

In that play <Term a="martians">Martians</Term> and anti-Martians never shared the board. Try to break this on two larger boards. On <Tex src="A_3"/> three cities stand in a row; on <Tex src="D_4"/> a central city is joined to three others. The goal on each board is to make one city negative while another is positive. After twenty moves the board tells you why you have not managed it.

The goal cannot be met. On every board of <Term a="finite">finite type</Term>, every root population is all Martians or all anti-Martians. There are <Tex src="2N"/> of them, where <Tex src="N=nh/2"/> for a board with $n$ cities and a whole number $h$, the Coxeter number, that depends on the board. Wildberger states this for boards with single roads <Cite k="W03a"/> ({'Proposition 3.3'}), with the remark that he knows no direct combinatorial proof; it is verified by computation for every board of finite type of rank at most eight. On <Tex src="A_3"/>, with <Tex src="n=3"/> and <Tex src="h={{types.A3.coxeter_number}}"/>, there are <G k="types.A3.n_roots"/> root populations, and <G k="types.A3.n_positive_roots"/> of them are positive.

<Beat n={3}>

A mutation can raise a city, lower it, or leave it unchanged. Compare the inflow, the total its neighbours send down their roads, with twice the city's own population. The mutation raises the city when the inflow is larger, leaves it unchanged when the two are equal, and lowers it when the inflow is smaller. In the words of Elek's notes <Cite k="Ele16"/>, a city is **unhappy** when a mutation would raise it, **happy** when a mutation would leave it unchanged, and **manic** when a mutation would lower it.

**Kostant's game** is attributed to Kostant; it was popularised by Allen Knutson <Cite k="Ele16"/> and by Alexander Postnikov's course at MIT <Cite k="Che17"/>. It starts from one Martian and mutates only <Term a="unhappy">unhappy</Term> cities. A move that raises a city is a **raising move**, and the game stops when no city is unhappy.

A worked play on <Tex src="A_3"/> from city <Tex src="1"/>. City <Tex src="2"/> is unhappy, since it receives one Martian and holds none; raising it gives <Tex src="(1,1,0)"/>. Then city <Tex src="3"/> is unhappy, and raising it gives <G k="kostant.A3.end_from_node[0]"/>. Now no city is unhappy, and the game stops there after <G k="kostant.A3.moves_from_node[0]"/> moves. On <Tex src="D_4"/> from city <Tex src="1"/>, raising moves at cities <Tex src="2"/>, <Tex src="3"/>, <Tex src="4"/> and <Tex src="2"/> pass through <Tex src="(1,1,0,0)"/>, <Tex src="(1,1,1,0)"/> and <Tex src="(1,1,1,1)"/> and stop at <G k="kostant.D4.end_from_node[0]"/> after <G k="kostant.D4.moves_from_node[0]"/> moves.

Before you play, predict whether the end depends on the starting city. The board below starts on <Tex src="A_3"/>; its chooser also offers <Tex src="D_4"/> and <Tex src="E_8"/>, and you can start from any city.

It does not depend on it. On <Tex src="E_8"/> the game stops at <G k="kostant.E8.end_from_node[0]"/> after <G k="kostant.E8.moves_from_node[0]"/> moves from every starting city, as computation confirms for each of them. This end is the **highest root** of the board: the root population at which no mutation raises any city. On every board of finite type with single roads, each <Term a="raising-move">raising move</Term> adds exactly one Martian, every play from one Martian has exactly <Tex src="h-2"/> moves, and every play ends at the <Term a="highest-root">highest root</Term>; this is verified by computation on every such board of rank at most eight, with random plays from every starting city. On <Tex src="E_8"/> the <Term a="coxeter-number">Coxeter number</Term> is <Tex src="h={{types.E8.coxeter_number}}"/>.

<Beat n={4}>

The <Term a="root-population">root populations</Term> of a board can be grown all at once. The Orbit Garden starts from the simple root populations and applies every mutation to everything it holds, one layer per press. On <Tex src="A_3"/> it stops at <G k="types.A3.n_roots"/> root populations, on <Tex src="D_4"/> at <G k="types.D4.n_roots"/>, and on <Tex src="E_8"/> at <G k="types.E8.n_roots"/>. Each count is the <Tex src="2N"/> of beat two: half of the root populations are positive, and the other half are their negatives. The three counts are verified by computation. These boards are of <Term a="finite">finite type</Term>; <ModuleThree /> names two other kinds, <Term a="affine">affine</Term> and <Term a="neither">neither</Term>.

Now keep only the positive root populations and arrange them by total population, with an edge wherever a mutation raises a city. On a board of finite type with single roads every such edge raises one city by exactly one, and the picture is the **root poset** of the board. Wildberger draws several of these posets <Cite k="W20"/> (section nine), with his own numbering of the cities of the <Tex src="D"/> and <Tex src="E"/> boards, which differs from the site's. At the top of the poset sits the highest root, and a play of <Term a="kostants-game">Kostant's game</Term> is a climb from the bottom of the poset to its top.

<Beat n={5}>

Not every garden stops. Join three cities in a triangle, each pair by one road each way. Grown for <G k="layer_growth_examples[label=triangle].layers"/> layers, the garden of the triangle holds <G k="layer_growth_examples[label=triangle].root_populations_found"/> root populations, with <G k="layer_growth_examples[label=triangle].new_per_layer[1]"/> new ones in every layer after the first, and the tool reports that it stopped after <G k="layer_growth_examples[label=triangle].layers"/> layers. That report says where the search was cut off, and nothing more.

The reason for the growth is a population that no mutation changes. In <G k="affine.A2.marks"/> every city receives one Martian from each of its two neighbours, exactly twice what it holds, so every city is <Term a="happy">happy</Term>. Add one Martian to any city of this population and the result is again a root population. Adding further copies gives larger and larger root populations, so the root populations of the triangle are unbounded. Wildberger proves this <Cite k="W20"/> ({'Proposition 6.1'}), and it is verified by computation. Which boards stop growing and which do not is the subject of <ModuleThree />.

<LieTheory>

A <Term a="population">population</Term> $p$ stands for the element $\sum_i p_i\alpha_i$ of the root lattice $Q$, written in simple-root coordinates. A <Term a="mutate">mutation</Term> at $i$ is the simple reflection $s_i(\beta)=\beta-\langle\alpha_i^\vee,\beta\rangle\alpha_i$, which changes only the coefficient $p_i$, into $p_i-\sum_j C_{ij}p_j$. Here $C$ is the Cartan matrix in the convention $C_{ij}=\langle\alpha_i^\vee,\alpha_j\rangle$ of Kac and SageMath; Bourbaki prints its transpose. The <Term a="root-population">root populations</Term> are the real roots, the <Term a="simple-root-population">simple root populations</Term> are the simple roots, the positive root populations are the positive roots, and the mutations generate the Weyl group $W$. For $C$ simply laced and of finite type, the positive roots arranged by height form the <Term a="root-poset">root poset</Term> with the highest root $\theta$ at the top, and <Term a="kostants-game">Kostant's game</Term> climbs it one simple root at a time.

</LieTheory>
