<Beat n={1}>

In module four the numbers game on <Tex src="A_3"/> from <Tex src="(1,1,1)"/> always stopped after the same number of moves, at the same position, whatever the order of firing. The start with one on every node is called <Tex src="\rho"/>.

Firing a node is a reflection: in free play, firing the same node twice gives back the position you had. The moves combine and undo, so they form a group, the **Weyl group** of the board. On <Tex src="A_3"/> it is the group of all ways to shuffle four things.

From <Tex src="\rho"/>, <Term a="legal-play">legal play</Term> reaches exactly <G k="types.A3.weyl_order"/> positions, one for each element of the group <Cite k="Don08"/> ({'Theorem 2.8'}). The textbook account is <Cite k="BB05"/> ({'section 4.3'}), which allows moves on negative numbers too; <Unverified item="E3" fallback="the site's statement follows Donnelly's theorem.">the main theorem of that section, believed to be its {'Theorem 4.3.1'}, states the same correspondence.</Unverified>

The Game Board below drives the Weyl Explorer under it. The explorer draws each reachable position as a dot, in layers by the number of moves, <G k="types.A3.length_distribution"/> dots per layer, and marks your game as a path.

<Beat n={2}>

A play is a **word** in the node labels. Two words can reach one position: <Tex src="1,3"/> and <Tex src="3,1"/> both lead to <Tex src="(-1,3,-1)"/>. The **length** of a position is the least number of moves that reaches it: the layer of its dot.

Each positive <Term a="root-population">root population</Term> gives a mirror, and <Tex src="\rho"/> lies on the positive side of every mirror. On <Tex src="A_3"/> a mirror **separates** a position from the start when the numbers on the nodes where its root has a <Tex src="1"/> add up to less than zero. After the play <Tex src="1,3,2"/> the position <Tex src="(2,-3,2)"/> is separated by the mirrors of <G k="types.A3.positive_roots[1]"/>, <G k="types.A3.positive_roots[3]"/> and <G k="types.A3.positive_roots[4]"/>, and lies on layer three.

The length of a position is always the number of mirrors that separate it from the start <Cite k="Hum90"/> ({'section 1.7'}). A node holds a positive number exactly when firing it crosses one more mirror, so a legal play crosses a new mirror at every move: it is a shortest word. The end <Tex src="(-1,-1,-1)"/> lies beyond every mirror, so each move uses up one positive root, and every complete game has <G k="types.A3.n_positive_roots"/> moves. The element every complete game reaches is the **longest element**, which sends <Tex src="\rho"/> to <Tex src="-\rho"/>.

</Beat>

<Beat n={4}>

Nodes <Tex src="1"/> and <Tex src="3"/> are not joined, so firing them in either order gives the same position. To forget such orders, draw a play as a stack of bricks: one column per node, each move dropping a brick that rests on the highest earlier brick in its own column or a joined one. The stack is the **heap** of the play; Wildberger calls it a frame <Cite k="W-MS"/> ({'pp. 45-46'}).

Swapping neighbouring moves at unjoined nodes keeps the <Term a="heap">heap</Term>, and the plays with a given heap are exactly the orders in which its bricks can be laid down, each after those beneath it <Cite k="Ste96"/> ({'Proposition 2.2'}). The plays <Tex src="1,3,2"/> and <Tex src="3,1,2"/> share a heap that no other play has. The <G k="heaps.A3.complete_games"/> complete games on <Tex src="A_3"/> make <G k="heaps.A3.distinct_heaps"/> different heaps, holding <G k="heaps.A3.games_per_heap"/> games, as verified by computation.

<T08WeylExplorer slot="figure" client:visible id="b5" preset="heap" board="A3" caption="The play 1, 3, 2 as a heap of bricks; load 3, 1, 2 with its button and the heap stays the same: two plays share it." />
</Beat>

<Beat n={5}>

Now play with letters: put <Tex src="x_1,x_2,x_3"/> on the nodes and fire along <G k="types.A3.longest_word" as="plain"/>. The first move fires <Tex src="x_1"/> and leaves <Tex src="x_1+x_2"/> on node <Tex src="2"/>, which the second move fires. The six moves fire <Tex src="x_1,\ x_1+x_2,\ x_2,\ x_1+x_2+x_3,\ x_2+x_3,\ x_3"/>: each positive root population of <Tex src="A_3"/>, written with letters, exactly once.

On any board the forms fired are the positive root populations of the **reversed board**, the board with every road turned round <Cite k="Don08"/> ({'Remark 2.13 and Theorem 5.2'}). Donnelly fires positive numbers, as this site does; his matrix is the transpose of the Cartan matrix used here. A board with single roads, such as <Tex src="A_3"/>, is its own reversed board. From an all-positive start, the numbers fired are these forms evaluated at the start.

<Beat n={6}>

From <Tex src="\rho"/> each number fired is the sum of the coefficients of a positive root of the reversed board. These roots are the **coroots** of the board, and the sum is the **height** of the coroot. On <Tex src="A_3"/> and <Tex src="E_8"/> a coroot and its root have the same height; with double roads they can differ, so the numbers fired are heights of coroots.

Make a **tally** of how often each value is fired. On <Tex src="A_3"/> the game fires <G k="types.A3.fired_values" as="plain"/>: the value <Tex src="1"/> <G k="types.A3.fired_tally.1"/> times, <Tex src="2"/> twice, <Tex src="3"/> once. Draw the tally as bars and read them sideways: row <Tex src="k"/> holds the bars of height at least <Tex src="k"/>. The row lengths, in increasing order, are the **exponents** <G k="types.A3.exponents"/>, and <Tex src="({{types.A3.exponents[0]}}+1)({{types.A3.exponents[1]}}+1)({{types.A3.exponents[2]}}+1)={{types.A3.weyl_order}}"/>, the number of positions of beat one.

On <Tex src="E_8"/> the game has <G k="types.E8.n_positive_roots"/> moves. The value <Tex src="1"/> is fired <G k="types.E8.fired_tally.1"/> times and the value <G k="types.E8.exponents[7]"/> <G k="types.E8.fired_tally.29"/> time. Read sideways, the tally gives the exponents <G k="types.E8.exponents" as="plain"/>, and the order of the Weyl group is

<Tex display src="\begin{gathered}({{types.E8.exponents[0]}}+1)({{types.E8.exponents[1]}}+1)({{types.E8.exponents[2]}}+1)({{types.E8.exponents[3]}}+1)\\({{types.E8.exponents[4]}}+1)({{types.E8.exponents[5]}}+1)({{types.E8.exponents[6]}}+1)({{types.E8.exponents[7]}}+1)\\={{types.E8.weyl_order}}\end{gathered}"/>

that is, <G k="types.E8.weyl_order"/>. Steinberg recorded this pattern for roots without a proof <Cite k="Ste59"/> ({'9.1'}); Kostant gave the first proof for all boards of finite type at once <Cite k="Kos59"/>, and Macdonald another <Cite k="Mac72"/>. The version for coroots, which the game gives, was verified by computation for every board of rank at most eight.

<Beat n={7}>

Colour the nodes with two colours so that joined nodes differ; on <Tex src="A_3"/>, nodes <Tex src="1"/> and <Tex src="3"/> take one colour and node <Tex src="2"/> the other. The nodes of one colour form a **colour class**. From <Tex src="\rho"/>, fire every node of one class, then every node of the other, and repeat; firing one class is one **round**.

On a board with at least two nodes every firing of this **wave** is legal, and the game ends after the same number of rounds whichever colour starts <Cite k="FZ03b"/> ({'Lemma 2.1'}); <Cite k="Ste59"/> ({'6.3'}). That number is called <Tex src="h"/>, and it is one more than the largest exponent. Predict it on <Tex src="A_3"/> before you play below.

On <Tex src="A_3"/> the rounds pass through <Tex src="(-1,3,-1)"/>, <Tex src="(2,-3,2)"/>, <Tex src="(-2,1,-2)"/> and <Tex src="(-1,-1,-1)"/>: <G k="types.A3.coxeter_number"/> rounds and <G k="types.A3.n_positive_roots"/> moves. On <Tex src="E_8"/> the wave takes <G k="types.E8.coxeter_number"/> rounds, and <Tex src="{{types.E8.exponents[7]}}+1={{types.E8.coxeter_number}}"/>.

<Beat n={8}>

Each position <Tex src="\lambda"/> with no negative number labels a **representation** <Tex src="V(\lambda)"/>, a space on which the Lie algebra of the board acts; module eight builds both. Its **dimension** comes from two games.

Record the word of a complete game from <Tex src="\rho"/> and the product <Tex src="D"/> of the numbers fired. Replay the word from <Tex src="\lambda+\rho"/> and let <Tex src="M"/> be the product of the numbers fired there. Then <Tex src="\dim V(\lambda)=M/D"/>: Weyl's dimension formula. On <Tex src="A_2"/> with <Tex src="\lambda=(1,1)"/> the word <Tex src="1,2,1"/> fires <Tex src="1,2,1"/> from <Tex src="\rho"/>, so <Tex src="D=2"/>, and <Tex src="2,4,2"/> from <Tex src="\lambda+\rho=(2,2)"/>, so <Tex src="M=16"/>. The quotient is <G k="weyl_dimension[type=A2;highest_weight=1,1].dimension"/>. The recipe was verified by computation on <G k="weyl_dimension" as="count"/> representations, from dimension <G k="weyl_dimension[type=A2;highest_weight=1,0].dimension"/> on <Tex src="A_2"/> to <G k="weyl_dimension[type=E8;highest_weight=1,0,0,0,0,0,0,0].dimension"/> on <Tex src="E_8"/>.

<LieTheory>

Positions are weights in fundamental-weight coordinates. The explorer draws the Hasse diagram of the left weak order on <Tex src="W"/>: the play <Tex src="i_1,\dots,i_k"/> reaches <Tex src="w=s_{i_k}\cdots s_{i_1}"/>, whose separating roots form the inversion set of <Tex src="w^{-1}"/> <Cite k="Hum90"/>. Legal plays are reduced words, complete ones those of <Tex src="w_0"/>. Heaps are those of Viennot and Stembridge; the plays with one heap form a commutation class <Cite k="Ste96"/>. The tally is the theorem of Shapiro, Steinberg, Kostant and Macdonald on heights and exponents <Cite k="Ste59"/>, for the dual root system. The rounds count the Coxeter number <Tex src="h=m_n+1"/>. With <Tex src="c_0"/>, <Tex src="c_1"/> the products of the reflections of the two colour classes, the wave is the alternating product <Tex src="\cdots c_1c_0"/> of <Tex src="h"/> factors, a reduced word of <Tex src="w_0"/> <Cite k="FZ03b"/>: for even <Tex src="h"/> a power of the bipartite Coxeter element <Tex src="c_1c_0"/>, for odd <Tex src="h"/> not. Beat eight computes <Tex src="\dim V(\lambda)=\prod_{\beta>0}\langle\lambda+\rho,\beta^\vee\rangle/\langle\rho,\beta^\vee\rangle"/>. Here <Tex src="C_{ij}=\langle\alpha_i^\vee,\alpha_j\rangle"/>; Bourbaki and Humphreys print the transpose.

</LieTheory>
