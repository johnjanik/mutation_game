<Beat n={1}>

In the mutation game, N. J. Wildberger's game of Martians on a board <Cite k="W20"/>, every move changes one city. This lesson looks for something that no move changes. Give each population $p$ of a board a single number, its <Term a="form-value">**form value**</Term>, written $(p,p)$. On a board with single roads, where each line of the drawing is one road each way, the recipe is

<Tex display src="\begin{aligned} (p,p) \;=\;& 2\sum_{i} p_i^2 \\ &-\, 2\!\!\sum_{\text{joined pairs } \{i,j\}}\!\! p_i\,p_j . \end{aligned}"/>

The second sum counts each pair of joined cities once.

Take the board <Tex src="A_3"/> of the earlier lessons, three cities in a row, with one Martian in city <Tex src="1"/>. The squares add up to <Tex src="1"/> and no joined pair has two occupied cities, so the form value is <Tex src="2\cdot 1-0=2"/>. The "energy meter" below the board shows this number. Before you play, predict what it will read after city <Tex src="2"/> mutates. The board waits for your answer.

City <Tex src="2"/> receives the Martian of city <Tex src="1"/>, and the population becomes <Tex src="(1,1,0)"/>. Now the squares add up to <Tex src="2"/>, and the one occupied pair contributes <Tex src="1\cdot 1"/>, so the form value is <Tex src="2\cdot 2-2\cdot 1=2"/> again. Play on as long as you like: the meter never moves. A quantity that no mutation changes is called **invariant**, and the form value is invariant on every board with single roads. Wildberger proves this <Cite k="W20"/> ({'Proposition 7.1'}), and it is verified by computation for every board of finite type of rank at most eight.

One Martian in any city has form value <Tex src="2"/>, and every root population is reached from one Martian by mutations. So every root population has form value <Tex src="2"/>: all <G k="types.A3.n_roots"/> of them on <Tex src="A_3"/>, and every root population of every board with single roads.

<Beat n={2}>

Where does the recipe come from? Build a conserved quantity yourself, on the board <Tex src="A_2"/> of two joined cities. Choose a weight for each city, <Tex src="w_1"/> and <Tex src="w_2"/>, and a weight <Tex src="w_{12}"/> for the joined pair, and form

<Tex display src="Q_w(p) \;=\; w_1\,p_1^2 + w_2\,p_2^2 - w_{12}\,p_1 p_2 ."/>

The button Test asks the engine whether every mutation leaves $Q_w$ unchanged. When the answer is no, the tool shows a **witness**: a population and a city whose mutation changes $Q_w$.

The builder starts with city weights <Tex src="2"/> and <Tex src="2"/> and road weight <Tex src="1"/>. These fail. One Martian in city <Tex src="1"/> gives <Tex src="Q_w(1,0)=2"/>; after city <Tex src="2"/> mutates, the population <Tex src="(1,1)"/> gives <Tex src="Q_w(1,1)=2+2-1=3"/>.

Change the weights until the test passes. It passes exactly when all three weights are equal. With every weight equal to $w$, $Q_w$ is the form value of beat one multiplied by <Tex src="w/2"/>, so up to a common factor the recipe is forced. On a connected board, a pairing of populations that every mutation preserves is unique up to a constant <Cite k="W20"/> ({'Theorems 8.4 and 8.7'}). On a board with single roads the forced recipe is the one of beat one: weight <Tex src="2"/> on every city and <Tex src="2"/> on every joined pair, times a common factor.

</Beat>

<Beat n={3}>

The form value comes from a pairing of two populations, $(p,q)=p^{\mathsf T}Bq$, and $(p,p)$ is the pairing of $p$ with itself. On a board with single roads the matrix is <Tex src="B=2I-A"/>, where $A$ is the adjacency matrix: $A_{ij}$ counts the roads from city $i$ to city $j$. This is the **Cartan matrix** <Tex src="C=2I-A^{\mathsf T}"/> of module zero, because on such a board $A$ is symmetric. Its diagonal entries are <Tex src="2"/>, the form value of one Martian.

A mutation is a reflection for this form. Pair a population $p$ with one Martian in city $i$, the population $e_i$: the result $(p,e_i)$ is twice the population of city $i$ minus the populations of its neighbours. The mutation at $i$ subtracts exactly this amount from city $i$,

<Tex display src="S_i\,p \;=\; p-(p,e_i)\,e_i ,"/>

which is the rule of module one. In linear algebra a reflection fixes a mirror and reverses the direction across it. Here the mirror holds the populations with <Tex src="(p,e_i)=0"/>, which the mutation leaves alone, and $e_i$ itself is sent to $-e_i$. On any board the formula reads <Tex src="S_ip=p-\frac{ 2(p,e_i) }{ (e_i,e_i) }\,e_i"/>; with single roads $(e_i,e_i)$ is <Tex src="2"/> and the fraction is $(p,e_i)$.

<Beat n={4}>

A pairing gives lengths and angles, as the dot product does. Read $(p,p)$ as the square of the length of $p$, and the angle $\theta$ between $p$ and $q$ from $(p,q)=|p|\,|q|\cos\theta$. One Martian in any city has squared length <Tex src="2"/>, so every city has the same length <Tex src="\sqrt2"/>. Two joined cities have $(e_i,e_j)$ equal to <Tex src="-1"/>, so <Tex src="\cos\theta=-1/2"/> and the angle between them is <Tex src="120^\circ"/>. Two cities that are not joined have $(e_i,e_j)$ equal to <Tex src="0"/>: they are perpendicular. On <Tex src="A_3"/>, cities <Tex src="1"/> and <Tex src="3"/> are perpendicular, and each makes <Tex src="120^\circ"/> with city <Tex src="2"/>.

The picture draws <Tex src="A_2"/> in the plane so that the form is the ordinary dot product. The two cities are arrows of length <Tex src="\sqrt2"/> at <Tex src="120^\circ"/>, and all <G k="types.A2.n_roots"/> root populations are arrows of that same length, since every root population has form value <Tex src="2"/>. A later tool, <Kaleidoscope />, will replace this picture with one you can play on.

<Beat n={5}>

Every root population has form value <Tex src="2"/>. Is every population of form value <Tex src="2"/> a root population? On a board of finite type with single roads, yes. In the form that has been checked: the positive root populations are exactly the populations with no anti-Martians and form value <Tex src="2"/>, verified by computation for every such board of rank at most eight, for the populations between zero and the highest root. Since $(-p,-p)=(p,p)$ and the negative of a root population is again one, the same holds for populations with no Martians. Wildberger states the converse for every whole-number population of such a board <Cite k="W03a"/> ({'Proposition 3.5'}), including those that mix Martians and anti-Martians.

Test candidates in the table below. Before you type, predict whether <Tex src="(1,-1,0)"/>, which mixes the two kinds, is a root population of <Tex src="A_3"/>.

The statement is for boards of finite type only, and <ModuleThree /> says which boards those are. Join three cities in a triangle and add a tail: city <Tex src="3"/> joined to city <Tex src="4"/>, and city <Tex src="4"/> to city <Tex src="5"/>. This board is not of finite type. On it the population <Tex src="(1,1,1,0,1)"/> has form value <Tex src="2\cdot 4-2\cdot 3=2"/>, since the triangle holds three occupied pairs and the tail none, and yet it is not a root population. This is verified by computation, and the second table shows the engine's verdict.

<LieTheory>

The form value is the Tits form of the graph, equivalently the Killing form restricted to the root lattice, up to scale; Wildberger works with it under the name $Q$ <Cite k="W20"/> (section seven). In general the pairing is the invariant bilinear form $(\ ,\ )$ with <Tex src="(\alpha_i,\alpha_i)=2d_i"/>, and on a simply laced board every $d_i$ is <Tex src="1"/>. The matrix <Tex src="2I-A"/> is the Cartan matrix of a simply laced type, symmetric, in the convention $C_{ij}=\langle\alpha_i^\vee,\alpha_j\rangle$ of Kac and SageMath; Bourbaki prints its transpose, which here is the same matrix. A <Term a="mutate">mutation</Term> is the simple reflection <Tex src="s_i(\beta)=\beta-\frac{ 2(\beta,\alpha_i) }{ (\alpha_i,\alpha_i) }\alpha_i"/>, the <Term a="root-population">root populations</Term> are the roots, and on a simply laced finite type the roots are exactly the vectors of the root lattice of squared length <Tex src="2"/>.

</LieTheory>
