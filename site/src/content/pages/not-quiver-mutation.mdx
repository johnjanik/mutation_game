On this site a board never changes. A mutation at a city changes the population of that city and nothing else. Firing a node in the numbers game changes numbers only. The mutation game is Wildberger's <Cite k="W20"/>. The <Term a="numbers-game">numbers game</Term> is due to Mozes <Cite k="Moz90"/>; Proctor had used the same process earlier <Cite k="Pro84"/>, and Eriksson developed it <Cite k="Eri95a"/>. Several neighbouring games, and one operation with the same name, behave differently. This page sets them side by side.

## Kostant's game

Kostant's game is attributed to Kostant; <Cite k="Ele16"/> and <Cite k="Che17"/> describe it and its moods. It is the <Term a="mutation-game">mutation game</Term> with a ratchet: start from one Martian in one <Term a="city">city</Term>, and let a city mutate only when the mutation raises its <Term a="population">population</Term>. Whether it may is read off the other view through the translator <Tex src="a=Cp"/>. The number on node $i$ is the amount by which a mutation would lower city $i$. The three moods of a city are the three signs of $a_i$: unhappy when <Tex src="a_i<0"/>, and only then may it <Term a="mutate">mutate</Term>; happy when <Tex src="a_i=0"/>; manic, or excited, when <Tex src="a_i>0"/>.

On a simply laced <Term a="board">board</Term> of finite type every move raises one city by exactly one Martian, and every population met is a positive <Term a="root-population">root population</Term>: both verified by computation for every simply laced board of finite type of rank at most eight. Over all starting cities the moves join exactly the pairs of positive root populations that differ by one Martian in one city, so the state graph of the game is the Hasse diagram of the positive root populations. The argument uses the form value. On such a board the positive root populations are exactly the populations with no negative entry and form value <Tex src="2"/> <Cite k="W03a"/> ({'Proposition 3.5'}), and adding one Martian to city $i$ of a population $p$ adds <Tex src="2a_i+2"/> to its form value. So for a positive root population $p$, the population <Tex src="p+e_i"/>, with one more Martian in city $i$, is a positive <Term a="root-population">root population</Term> exactly when <Tex src="a_i=-1"/>, which is when a mutation at city $i$ raises $p$ by one Martian. Removing one Martian from city $i$ adds <Tex src="2-2a_i"/> instead. Since the form value of $p$ is <Tex src="\sum_i p_ia_i"/>, which is positive, some city with <Tex src="p_i>0"/> has <Tex src="a_i\ge1"/>, and as the form is positive definite, <Tex src="a_i=1"/> there unless $p$ is one Martian. So every <Term a="positive-root-population">positive root population</Term> other than one Martian lies one Martian above another, and every positive root population is met from some starting city. On a board that is not of finite type one raising move may add more than one Martian to a city, even with single roads only: on the triangle, three cities each joined to the other two by single roads, mutating city {'3'} turns <Tex src="(1,1,0)"/> into <Tex src="(1,1,2)"/>.

## Chip-firing

Chip-firing is a different game. Each node holds a pile of chips, and a node fires when its pile reaches its degree: it sends one chip along each of its edges, so it always loses the same amount, and the total number of chips never changes. Each firing adds a fixed vector to the piles, so firings commute: the order of two firings does not change the result.

In the numbers game the sign of the number on a <Term a="node">node</Term> decides whether it may fire; the node then sends out amounts proportional to that number, and the number changes sign. Firings at joined nodes do not commute, since the corresponding mutations do not <Cite k="W20"/> ({'Theorem 5.1'}). On a board of <Term a="finite">finite</Term> type no weighted sum of the <Term a="numbers">numbers</Term> survives every firing: firing node $i$ keeps the sum <Tex src="\sum_j w_j a_j"/> of every position only when entry $i$ of <Tex src="C^{\mathsf T}w"/> is zero, and $C$ is invertible on such a board. What every move keeps is the <Term a="form-value">form value</Term> of the population $p$ with <Tex src="a=Cp"/>, whose entries may be fractions, verified by computation for every board of finite type of rank at most eight. The two games agree in the minuscule situation, where every number fired equals <Tex src="1"/>, after the change of variables <Tex src="v=1-u"/> of <Cite k="BKR16"/> ({'Remark 4.11'}). That paper fires negative numbers and writes the transpose of $C$: negate every number and transpose its matrix to read it in this site's convention, so its numbers equal to <Tex src="-1"/> are this site's numbers equal to <Tex src="1"/>.

## Quiver representations

Keep one of the two roads of each line of a simply laced board (choose a direction for each line), and the result is a **quiver**, with vertices and arrows. A representation of a <Term a="quiver">quiver</Term> puts a vector space at each vertex and a linear map on each arrow. At a vertex where every arrow points in, a reflection functor replaces the space there and turns the arrows at the vertex round. For an indecomposable representation of the quiver other than the simple one at that vertex, which has dimension one there and zero elsewhere, the dimension there changes exactly as the population of a city changes when that city mutates <Cite k="BGP73"/> ({'Theorem 1.1'}). Gabriel's theorem: when the underlying board is of type <Tex src="A"/>, <Tex src="D"/> or <Tex src="E"/>, the indecomposable representations correspond to the positive <Term a="root-population">root populations</Term> (<Cite k="BGP73"/>, {'Theorem 3.1'}; first proved in <Cite k="Gab72"/>).

A quiver arrow is an orientation; the unequal road counts of Wildberger's boards are lengths. A quiver only ever gives a symmetric Cartan matrix, while two <Term a="roads">roads</Term> one way and one road back mark a long root and a short one. The site keeps the two meanings of arrow apart.

## Cluster mutation: a different operation with the same name

Fomin and Zelevinsky call an operation on a quiver and its variables mutation. It changes the quiver as well as one variable, and it obeys another rule. In the table $N$ is the number of positive roots and $n$ the number of nodes.

## Common confusions

Each item below corrects a statement about the mutation game that is sometimes made.

<ul data-confusions>
<li data-confusion="board"><strong>A mutation does not change the board.</strong> A mutation at a city changes the population of that city and leaves the other cities and every road as they were <Cite k="W20"/> ({'sections 2 and 5'}). Rules that turn arrows round and add or remove arrows between neighbours belong to the quiver mutation of Fomin and Zelevinsky. The mutation game is not a combinatorial shadow of that operation; the two share a name.</li>
<li data-confusion="one-entry"><strong>A mutation changes one entry.</strong> It is <Tex src="p_i\mapsto p_i-\sum_j C_{ij}p_j"/>, and every other entry keeps its value. An update <Tex src="x\mapsto x-x_i\,C^{\mathsf T}e_i"/>, which negates entry <Tex src="i"/> and changes the entries of its neighbours, is the firing move of the numbers game on the reversed board, the board whose <Term a="cartan-matrix">Cartan matrix</Term> is <Tex src="C^{\mathsf T}"/>.</li>
<li data-confusion="simply-laced"><strong>On a simply laced board the two games still differ.</strong> There <Tex src="C"/> is symmetric and <Tex src="R_i=S_i^{\mathsf T}"/>: the two moves are transposes of each other, two different matrices at any node joined to another. The correct relations are <Tex src="CS_i=R_iC"/>, so that a population <Tex src="p"/> and the <Term a="position">position</Term> <Tex src="a=Cp"/> move together, and <Tex src="R_i(C)=S_i(C^{\mathsf T})^{\mathsf T}"/>. Both follow from writing the moves as matrices, <Tex src="S_i=I-e_ie_i^{\mathsf T}C"/> and <Tex src="R_i=I-Ce_ie_i^{\mathsf T}"/>, with $I$ the identity matrix and $e_i$ the population with one Martian in city $i$. The number on node <Tex src="i"/> is the amount by which a mutation would lower city <Tex src="i"/>. The claim that the mutation game on <Tex src="C"/> is the numbers game on <Tex src="C^{\mathsf T}"/> is true of the two actions on dual spaces, each the adjoint of the other, and false as a claim that the two games have the same positions and the same moves.</li>
<li data-confusion="sign"><strong>A mutation needs no sign condition.</strong> Any city may mutate at any time. Conditions define variants: only <Term a="raising-move">raising moves</Term> give <Term a="kostants-game">Kostant's game</Term>, and on the numbers side only positive firings give <Term a="legal-play">legal play</Term>.</li>
</ul>
